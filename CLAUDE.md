## Voice

### The Five Rules

1. Speak like a knowledgeable local, not a tourism board. Insider specificity over polished promotion.
2. Lead with feeling, earn the practical. Inspiration before information.
3. Be specific, always. Real names, real places, real products, real people.
4. Write for the curious, not the cautious. Readers want to go deeper; give them a reason.
5. Never sell, always invite. Share and recommend; never pitch or convince.

### The Narrator

Write like a tour guide who is also the most interesting person at the dinner table. Someone who knows the real story behind everything, finds it genuinely delightful, and leans in when the detail is especially good. Warm, funny, authoritative. Treats the reader as an intelligent adult who can handle complexity, irony, and the occasional uncomfortable historical fact delivered without flinching.

The energy: your most knowledgeable friend giving you the actual story, not the sanitized version.

Both registers are available to the same voice: the narrator who notes the irony of the Aurora Inn's biography also names the village of Chonodote and its 1,500 peach trees with care. Dry is fine. A little wry is fine. Gossip-adjacent is welcome when the subject warrants it.

### The Narrator Is Not

- Precious or self-congratulatory about the region
- A local who resents visitors
- A marketer trying to close a booking
- A historian who forgot that people have to want to keep reading

### The Upstate Test

Before submitting any copy: **Would a knowledgeable local say this to a friend they trusted?** If yes, ship it. If it sounds like a brochure, rewrite it.

## Language Rules

### Hard Bans (these never appear)

- **No em dashes** anywhere. Use commas, colons, or periods.
- **No exclamation marks.** Enthusiasm lives in specificity, not punctuation.
- **No negation framing.** Never establish a place as what it isn't ("more than just wine country," "not your typical tourist town").
- **No passive constructions** unless genuinely unavoidable.

### Phrases to Watch

Not hard-banned, but never use as defaults or substitutes for a real observation. If it could appear in any tourism brochure about any destination, it hasn't earned its place. Replace with a specific fact, a real name, or an observation that could only be made about this exact location.

Watch list: hidden gem, nestled, charming, breathtaking views, quaint, rustic charm, off the beaten path, there's something for everyone, world-class, come experience, must-see, explore all that [X] has to offer, you won't be disappointed, immerse yourself, true gem

### Style

- Oxford comma, always
- Contractions are fine
- Numbers under 10: spell out. 10 and above: numerals.
- **Upstate** (capitalized) = the brand. **upstate** (lowercase) = the region.
- Trail names are proper nouns: the Finger Lakes Wine Trail, the Cayuga Lake Wine Trail
- **Haudenosaunee** (not Iroquois) for the Confederacy
- Nation names: Cayuga (Gayogo̱hó:nǫ'), Seneca (Onödowa'ga:'), Onondaga, Oneida, Mohawk, Tuscarora
- **Chonodote** named first, translated second: "Chonodote, which translates to Peachtown"
- Present tense for all descriptive content
- Sentence fragments allowed when they serve rhythm; use sparingly

## Narrative Framework

### Central Proposition

This land has been attracting people who are paying close attention for ten thousand years. The Haudenosaunee recognized the abundance first. The reform leaders chose this geography deliberately. Konstantin Frank looked at the cold Seneca slopes and saw Ukraine. Hermann Wiemer came from the Mosel and stopped. The traveler reading this guide is next in that line.

### Four Content Themes

1. **The land makes everything possible, and always has.** Glaciers created conditions, not scenery. Deep thermal lakes moderate climate. Shale soils stress vines exactly right. The Haudenosaunee built civilization on this abundance.
2. **Abundance creates the conditions for ideas.** Seneca Falls, Auburn, the reform network grew from communities that were fed and free to be restless. Cornell was built on Erie Canal wealth.
3. **People came here on purpose and stayed.** Every chapter involves someone who arrived, recognized what they were looking at, and committed. Name those people. Let their reasons be the story.
4. **The same geography, different centuries.** Wine trails run along the same shores the Haudenosaunee farmed. The reform corridor follows the old Seneca Turnpike. The geography is continuous even when the history is rupture.

## Chapter Template (nine sections, matching Cayuga)

1. **The lake, and what it keeps producing** — anchor identity. Lead with people and stories, not geography.
2. **At a glance** — stats table
3. **The towns** — portraits of anchor communities
4. **Heritage** — Haudenosaunee territory, specific historical events and people, landmark sites
5. **Wine trail** — terroir argument, named producers with editorial notes, how to run the route
6. **Farms, cider, and food** — farm corridor, specific operators, dining by town
7. **Outside** — gorges, waterfalls, hiking, water recreation, wildlife
8. **Where to stay** — independently owned preferred, each property gets a character note
9. **Getting here and getting around** — drive times, route notes, seasonal hours, passport check-ins

### Chapter Key Anchors

- **Seneca Lake:** Wine country spine, Watkins Glen gorge trail, Geneva, Dr. Frank's origin story at Hammondsport, Catherine's Town and the Sullivan march (western shore), Seneca Nation (Onödowa'ga:') territory
- **Keuka Lake:** Wine origin story (Bostwick's 1829 planting), Hammondsport, Bluff Point, Dr. Frank's Vinifera Wine Cellars (1962), Penn Yan, Y-shape geography
- **Canandaigua Lake:** Western wine country, Canandaigua town and lakefront, Naples valley, Bristol Mountain, Treaty of Canandaigua (1794), Haudenosaunee land rights context
- **Eastern Lakes:** Skaneateles (clearest lake, Syracuse's drinking water, no wine trail identity), Owasco (Auburn's lake, Ward O'Hara Agricultural Museum), Honeoye, Canadice, Hemlock, Conesus

### Itineraries to Write

| Itinerary | Route | Duration | Theme |
|---|---|---|---|
| **The Freedom Line** | Auburn to Seneca Falls to Waterloo to Geneva | 3 days | Heritage. The reform corridor as one connected network. |
| **Around Seneca** | Watkins Glen to Geneva (west shore), east shore return | 2 days | Wine. The Seneca Lake loop run properly. |
| **Gorge Country** | Ithaca base, Taughannock, Buttermilk/Treman, Watkins Glen | 2 days | Outdoors. The glacial work made walkable. |
| **The Cayuga Farm Loop** | Trumansburg south to Interlaken via Route 89 | 1 day | Farm and cider. Finger Lakes Cider House and Lively Run Dairy as anchors. |

**Itinerary routing discipline:** Single cardinal direction per day, no backtracking, all stops verified open, driving segments over 45 minutes require a named midpoint stop, breakfast and dinner named for each day.

## guide_listings.json

Lives in the project root. Updated incrementally as each chapter is written.

### Rules

- Every operator named in any chapter or itinerary gets an entry. No exceptions.
- One entry per operator. No duplicates.
- Pull full listing record from the master spreadsheet. Don't summarize or abbreviate existing fields.
- Keep entries alphabetical by `Name`.
- Check the file before writing a new chapter. If an operator is already listed, skip it.

### The Lake Tag

The only modification to existing Tourismo listing data: the `Region` field in `Tags` becomes an array containing both `"Finger Lakes Region"` and the lake-specific value.

Valid lake values: `"Cayuga Lake"`, `"Seneca Lake"`, `"Keuka Lake"`, `"Canandaigua Lake"`, `"Eastern Lakes"`

Operators not tied to a single lake keep `"Region": "Finger Lakes Region"` as a string (not array).

### Two Additional Fields

Appended to each listing object (outside Tags):

- `guide_chapter` — which chapter first names this operator (e.g., `"cayuga"`, `"seneca"`, `"freedom_line"`)
- `guide_section` — which of the nine sections (e.g., `"wine_trail"`, `"farms_cider_food"`, `"where_to_stay"`)

## Writing Workflow

Follow the build sequence in "Guide Architecture" above (Seneca next, then badge tracks, then remaining lakes).

### For Each Lake Chapter

1. Pull relevant county sheets from `upstate_listings_by_county.xlsx`
2. Cross-reference `fl_listings_tagged.json` for trail-tagged operators
3. Research the lake's history, anchor towns, notable people, and origin stories carrying the four content themes
4. Write the nine sections in order, using Cayuga as the structural and tonal model
5. Update `guide_listings.json` with every named operator
6. Run the quality checklist before saving final output

### For Each Itinerary

1. Map every stop before writing. Declare cardinal direction, verify no backtracking.
2. Verify every food and lodging stop is currently operating.
3. Calculate drive times. Flag any segment over 45 minutes.
4. Write the narrative. Answer the spine question: what does this route reveal that another route wouldn't?
5. Update `guide_listings.json` for every named stop.

### For Each Badge Track (Layer 3)

1. Identify all tagged operators across counties from `fl_listings_tagged.json`.
2. Write the collection page: trail identity, what ties these operators together, how to run the trail.
3. Write individual trail descriptions following the same voice rules as lake chapters.
4. Cross-reference lake chapters to avoid duplicating narrative, but link back.
5. Update `guide_listings.json` for any operators not already captured by lake chapters.
