## 2. EDITORIAL VOICE — THE UPSTATE STANDARD

### The five voice rules
1. Speak like a knowledgeable local, not a tourism board — insider specificity over polished promotion
2. Lead with feeling, earn the practical — inspiration before information, always
3. Be specific — always. Real names, real places, real products, real people
4. Write for the curious, not the cautious — readers want to go deeper; give them a reason
5. Never sell, always invite — share and recommend; never pitch or convince

### Brand personality
Upstate is: warm but not precious. Rooted but not sleepy. Local but not insular.

Think: the farmer who also reads widely. The innkeeper who knows every trail by name. The third-generation winemaker who just got discovered by Bon Appétit and hasn't changed a thing.

### The narrator voice for this guide
Write like a tour guide who is also the most interesting person at the dinner table. Someone who knows the real story behind everything, finds it genuinely delightful, and is not above leaning in when the detail is especially good. The voice is warm, funny, and authoritative. It treats the reader as an intelligent adult who can handle complexity, irony, and the occasional uncomfortable historical fact delivered without flinching.

The energy is: your most knowledgeable friend giving you the actual story, not the sanitized version. They will tell you that the man who sold Harriet Tubman her farm was technically breaking federal law and did it anyway. That the Aurora Inn was built by a New York Times co-founder who chose a village of 700 people. That a wine trail exists because a Ukrainian refugee recognized terrain he'd spent his career working. That a university's most famous song is about a specific lake in central New York and has been copied by more schools than any other alma mater in the country. They find all of this genuinely fun. They want you to find it fun too.

The narrator leans into the good story. They do not editorialize excessively. They trust that a precise fact delivered accurately is more interesting than embellishment. Dry is fine. A little wry is fine. Gossip-adjacent is welcome when the subject warrants it. What's not welcome: snark, cynicism, tourism-board flatness, or any voice that sounds like it was produced by a committee.

This narrator also handles serious history well. The Sullivan-Clinton Campaign gets full weight. The dispossession of the Haudenosaunee gets full weight. The Underground Railroad gets full weight. The narrator does not make light of any of this — they take it seriously and deliver it with specificity. The same narrator who notes the irony of the Aurora Inn's biography also names the village of Chonodote and its 1,500 peach trees with care. Both registers are available to the same voice.

### What this voice is not
- Not precious or self-congratulatory about the region
- Not a local who resents visitors
- Not a marketer trying to close a booking
- Not a historian who forgot that people have to want to keep reading

## 3. LANGUAGE RULES

### Phrases to avoid relying on
These are not hard bans — a word like "charming" can appear if it's doing real specific work. What's prohibited is using them as defaults or as substitutes for a real observation. If the phrase could appear in any tourism brochure about any destination, it hasn't earned its place here. Replace it with a specific fact, a real name, or an observation that could only be made about this exact location.

Phrases to watch: hidden gem, nestled, charming, breathtaking views, quaint, rustic charm, off the beaten path, there's something for everyone, world-class, come experience, must-see, explore all that [X] has to offer, you won't be disappointed, immerse yourself, true gem

### Hard rules — these never appear
- **No em dashes** anywhere in Upstate copy — use commas, colons, or periods instead
- **No exclamation marks** — enthusiasm lives in specificity, not punctuation
- **No negation framing** — never establish a place's identity as what it isn't ("more than just wine country," "not your typical tourist town")
- **No passive constructions** unless genuinely unavoidable

### Grammar and style
- Oxford comma — always
- Contractions are fine: "it's" not "it is"
- Numbers under 10: spell out; 10 and above: numerals
- **Upstate** (capitalized) = the brand; **upstate** (lowercase) = the region generically
- Trail names are always proper nouns: the Finger Lakes Wine Trail, the Cayuga Lake Wine Trail
- **Haudenosaunee** (not Iroquois) for the Confederacy
- Nation names: Cayuga (Gayogo̱hó:nǫ'), Seneca (Onödowa'ga:'), Onondaga, Oneida, Mohawk, Tuscarora
- **Chonodote** named first, translated second: "Chonodote — which translates to Peachtown"
- Present tense for all descriptive content
- Sentence fragments allowed when they serve rhythm; use sparingly

### The Upstate Test
Before submitting any copy: **Would a knowledgeable local say this to a friend they trusted?**
If yes, ship it. If it sounds like a brochure, rewrite it.

## 4. NARRATIVE FRAMEWORK — FINGER LAKES SPECIFIC

### The central proposition
This land has been attracting people who are paying close attention for ten thousand years. The Haudenosaunee recognized the abundance first. The reform movement leaders chose this geography deliberately. Konstantin Frank looked at the cold Seneca slopes and saw Ukraine. Hermann Wiemer came from the Mosel and stopped. The traveler reading this guide is next in that line.

### The four content themes
1. **The land makes everything possible, and always has.** The glaciers created conditions, not scenery. Deep thermal lakes moderate the climate. Shale soils stress vines exactly right. The Haudenosaunee built civilization on this abundance. The wine grows because the land was already doing what serious agricultural land does.

2. **Abundance creates the conditions for ideas.** Seneca Falls, Auburn, the reform network — these movements grew from communities that were fed and therefore free to be restless. Cornell was built on Erie Canal wealth. The connection between agricultural productivity and civic and intellectual life is not incidental here.

3. **People came here on purpose and stayed.** Every chapter of Finger Lakes history involves someone who arrived, recognized what they were looking at, and committed. Name those people specifically and let their reasons be the story.

4. **The same geography, different centuries.** The wine trails run along the same shores the Haudenosaunee farmed. The reform corridor follows the old Seneca Turnpike. The gorge trails run through hanging valleys that existed before the Sullivan campaign. The geography is continuous even when the history is rupture.

### The Aurora narrative (locked — Cayuga chapter, do not alter)
This sequence opens the Cayuga chapter and is locked. Do not alter, omit, or add to it:

Chonodote / Peachtown (Haudenosaunee, 1,500 peach trees, same microclimate as the wine trails today)
E.B. Morgan builds the Aurora Inn 1833 (co-founder, New York Times)
Henry Wells founds Wells College 1868 (co-founder, American Express and Wells Fargo)
Millard Fillmore attended the Cayuga Lake Academy in Aurora before his presidency
Pleasant Rowland graduates Wells College 1962, creates American Girl, sells to Mattel for $700 million in 1998, returns to Aurora and restores the collapsing village from 2001
Wells College closes June 2024
The Inns of Aurora remains

## 5. GUIDE STRUCTURE

### Complete (in FingerLakes_Guide_v1.docx)
- Part I: Finger Lakes Regional Landing Page
- Part II: Cayuga Lake Chapter (nine sections — the structural model for all subsequent chapters)

### Remaining chapters to build
Each uses the same nine-section structure as the Cayuga chapter:
1. The lake, and what it keeps producing (anchor identity — lead with people and stories, not geography)
2. At a glance (stats table)
3. The towns (portraits of anchor communities)
4. Heritage (Haudenosaunee territory, specific historical events and people, landmark sites)
5. Wine trail (terroir argument, named producers with editorial notes, how to run the route)
6. Farms, cider, and food (farm corridor, specific operators, dining by town)
7. Outside (gorges, waterfalls, hiking, water recreation, wildlife)
8. Where to stay (independently owned preferred, each property gets a character note)
9. Getting here and getting around (drive times, route notes, seasonal hours, passport check-ins)

**Seneca Lake** — wine country spine, Watkins Glen and the gorge trail, Geneva, Dr. Frank's origin story at Hammondsport, Catherine's Town and the Sullivan march along the western shore, Haudenosaunee territory of the Seneca Nation

**Keuka Lake** — wine origin story (Bostwick's 1829 planting), Hammondsport, Bluff Point, Dr. Frank's Vinifera Wine Cellars (1962), Penn Yan, the Y-shape geography and what it produces in terms of lake views

**Canandaigua Lake** — western wine country, Canandaigua town and the lakefront, Naples valley, Bristol Mountain, the Canandaigua Treaty of 1794 and the Haudenosaunee land rights context

**Eastern Lakes** — Skaneateles and its village character (clearest lake, Syracuse's drinking water, no wine trail identity), Owasco (Auburn's lake, Ward O'Hara Agricultural Museum), Honeoye, Canadice, Hemlock, Conesus

### Itineraries to write
Full narratives for Tourismo pages, linked from the regional landing page:

- **The Freedom Line** — Auburn to Seneca Falls to Waterloo to Geneva, 3 days, heritage. The reform corridor as one connected network, not a checklist.
- **Around Seneca** — Watkins Glen to Geneva on the west shore, east shore return, 2 days, wine. The Seneca Lake loop run properly, both shores.
- **Gorge Country** — Ithaca base, Taughannock, Buttermilk/Treman, Watkins Glen, 2 days, outdoors. The glacial work made walkable.
- **The Cayuga Farm Loop** — Trumansburg south to Interlaken via Route 89, 1 day, farm and cider. The Finger Lakes Cider House and Lively Run Dairy as anchors.

Itinerary routing discipline: single cardinal direction per day, no backtracking, all stops verified open, driving segments over 45 minutes require a named midpoint stop, breakfast and dinner named for each day.

Every operator named by name in any chapter or itinerary must be tracked in `guide_listings.json` in the project root. Updated incrementally as each chapter is written.

### Format
Listings follow the standard Tourismo listing format exactly — the same field structure used in `upstate_listings_by_county.xlsx` and `fl_listings_tagged.json`. No new schema. ### The lake tag — the only modification to existing listing data
The `Region` field in `Tags` is the only field that changes from the base Tourismo format. It becomes an array containing both `"Finger Lakes Region"` and the lake-specific value.

Valid lake values to add:
- `"Cayuga Lake"` — operators on or primarily serving Cayuga Lake
- `"Seneca Lake"` — operators on or primarily serving Seneca Lake
- `"Keuka Lake"` — operators on or primarily serving Keuka Lake
- `"Canandaigua Lake"` — operators on or primarily serving Canandaigua Lake
- `"Eastern Lakes"` — Skaneateles, Owasco, Honeoye, Canadice, Hemlock, Conesus
- Operators not tied to a single lake keep `"Region": "Finger Lakes Region"` as a string (not array)


### JSON management rules
- Pull the full listing record from the master spreadsheet when adding a new entry — don't summarize or abbreviate existing fields
- One entry per operator — no duplicates
- Check the file before writing a new chapter — if an operator is already listed, do not add them again
- Keep entries in alphabetical order by `Name`
- Every named dining, lodging, heritage, farm, winery, and attraction operator gets an entry — no exceptions
- Do not modify any Tourismo field values other than converting `Region` to an array to add the lake tag
